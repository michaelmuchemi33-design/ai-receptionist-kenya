SALES PROCESS · FOLLOW-UP

From Customer Enquiry to Sale: A Better Follow-Up System for Kenyan Businesses

A customer asks for a price on Monday, says they will think about it and then gets busy. By Friday, nobody remembers who was meant to follow up. This is not always a marketing problem. Sometimes the business simply needs a clearer way to record what a customer asked for and what should happen next.

TWIGA Editorial Team·7 min read·22 November 2026

A sale rarely happens in one step

Many Kenyan businesses receive enquiries from several places: phone calls, WhatsApp, walk-ins, social pages and referrals. A customer may ask for a quotation, check availability, request a booking or compare two options. The team may answer well in the moment, but if the details are not recorded, the next step can be lost.

This is especially common in businesses where the owner handles sales as well as operations. The same person may be serving a customer, checking stock, arranging a delivery and answering calls. A follow-up that depends on memory is easy to miss when the day gets busy.

A more reliable process starts by capturing the question and assigning a next step. An AI receptionist can help with the first phone conversation by answering approved questions, collecting enquiry details and recording an order or booking request when the required integrations are available. The team remains responsible for decisions that need judgement.

Record enough context to make the next contact useful

A useful enquiry record does not need to be complicated. It should identify the customer, their contact details, what they asked about, the product or service they are interested in and what they expect next. If they asked for a quote, record which quote. If they asked for a callback, note the reason and the best time they gave.

Context is what makes follow-up feel personal rather than mechanical. “Call this person” is not as useful as “They asked whether the delivery charge to Kiambu is included in the quote and want confirmation before ordering.” The second note tells the team what to check before contacting the customer.

Ask only for details needed to respond. Explain why they are being collected, keep the information secure and respect a customer's choice about follow-up. Do not add a customer to marketing messages simply because they once made an enquiry. Promotional messages need an appropriate permission and opt-out process.

Answer accurately before asking for a decision

The first response should answer the customer's question with facts the business has approved. If they ask for a price, use the current catalogue or price list. If they ask about stock, check a reliable stock record where one is connected. If they ask about an appointment, check the diary only when the booking calendar is connected and configured.

When the information is not available, say so. A short message that the team will confirm is better than an invented answer. This is particularly important for delivery dates, special discounts, product availability, payment status and appointment times. Trust is harder to rebuild after a business promises something it cannot deliver.

Once the customer has enough information, the next step might be a quotation, a meeting, a booking or an order. Repeat the details before creating a record. For a booking, confirm the service and time. For an order, confirm the items and quantities. Keep payment status separate from order status so that nobody mistakes an unpaid request for money received.

Separate an enquiry, an order and a paid sale

A customer enquiry is not automatically an order. An order is not automatically paid. A paid order is not necessarily delivered. These distinctions sound simple, but they help staff speak accurately and make better decisions. The business should define what each stage means and who is allowed to move a record forward.

For example, a customer may ask for a dozen items and request delivery later in the week. The team can record the request, confirm stock and delivery conditions, then tell the customer what is actually available. Payment should be marked only when it has been confirmed through the business's payment process. Dispatch should be recorded only when the goods have left the business.

The AI should never claim that a payment, refund, delivery or message has been completed unless the connected action confirms it.

Follow up with a plan, not a flood of messages

Follow-up works best when it answers a real question or reminds someone about an agreed next step. If a customer asked for a quotation, send the quotation or explain what is still needed. If they wanted to check a service, answer that question. If they asked you to call at a particular time, respect that request.

Agree on a reasonable follow-up window and assign an owner. Avoid sending repeated messages just because a record remains open. If the customer says they are not interested or asks not to be contacted, record that preference and stop promotional follow-up. Give staff a simple way to see which enquiries need attention today and which are waiting on a customer.

The tone matters. A helpful follow-up sounds like a person remembering the conversation, not a sales script being pushed at everyone. A short note that mentions the actual service, product or quote is usually more useful than a generic message asking whether the customer is ready to buy.

Use the tools your team already relies on

You do not need to replace every app to improve follow-up. Start with the tools that contain the real information: your catalogue or price list, the booking diary, the order record and the customer contact list. Decide which of those TWIGA should read or update, then connect the provider and test the action with a staff member before relying on it.

If your team uses WhatsApp, social channels or email, keep track of which connections are actually enabled. A connection icon or saved API key does not always mean messages can be read or sent. Some platforms allow publishing but not inbox automation; others require approved templates or a customer messaging window. The workflow should reflect what the provider supports.

A short weekly review helps keep records useful. Look at open quotes, unconfirmed bookings, unpaid orders, requests waiting for stock checks and follow-ups that are overdue. Close records that are no longer active. Update prices and product availability so the receptionist and staff are not working from old information.

Measure the gaps in the customer journey

Measure the stages, not only the final sales total. How many enquiries came in? How many received a first response? How many requested a quote or appointment? How many became orders? How many orders were paid? How many customers needed a human handover? These numbers show where the process is breaking down.

A high number of enquiries with few quotations may indicate that customers need clearer product information. Many quotes with few follow-ups may point to a handover or workload issue. Orders that remain unpaid may need a clearer payment process. Use those patterns to improve the next step rather than assuming the answer is always to send more marketing.

Compare similar periods and take account of seasonality, promotions, stock levels and staff changes. A reporting dashboard can help, but it should be based on accurate records. No software can reliably measure a process if staff routinely skip the records or mark work as complete before it is done.

Build the process around the people doing the work

A better enquiry-to-sale system is a combination of useful information, accurate records and people who know what to do next. Automation can reduce the repetitive first-response work, but your team still owns the customer relationship, pricing decisions, special requests and service quality.
